There was a time when the person who sold you something had spent their entire life learning to judge it.

Before sunrise. The yard.

3am. The trucks have arrived.

The first sack comes off. It is opened, checked by hand. The smell tells you more than the colour.

The scale is set. The weights are placed. Every reading goes into the ledger.

By the time the market opens, the serious decisions have already been made. Quietly. Without an audience.

The mandi. Sacks weighed. Decisions made.

The bahi-khata recorded every transaction. Every weight. Every decision.

There was no undo. You got the quality right the first time, because your name was the only currency that actually mattered.

An elderly man walked into the office with his great-grandson. During the conversation, he asked where the family was from.

When he heard the answer, he paused. Then he told us that the provisions for his own wedding — decades ago — had come from this family.

He remembered. Not because anyone asked him to. Because the transaction had been done right.

No rating system produced that memory. No loyalty programme kept it alive. It survived because the original work was honest.

Word of mouth is the oldest algorithm. It still runs on the same input: did you do the work right?

My grandfather opened his ledger before sunrise in Jasrapur. He weighed grain on brass scales, wrote every transaction by hand, and built a reputation that travelled further than he ever did.

My father carried those standards into a spice business. Kalu Masaley Wala, 1990. Same discipline, different product. He taught me that a name is not something you inherit. It is something you re-earn every morning.

Today, a ledger sits beside a laptop. Conversations happen over a phone. Procurement reaches further than a bicycle ever could.

But the first thing I do each morning is the same thing my grandfather did.

Open a sack. Check the quality. Decide.

The tools changed.

The judgment didn't.

Before the first transaction, there is always a moment of stillness.

Convenience improved. Standards quietly declined.

Not because anyone chose to lower them. But because the people who once stood between the source and the kitchen — the merchants who staked their name on what they sold — gradually disappeared.

They were replaced by supply chains. By packaging that made promises the contents didn't keep.

The person who knew the product was replaced by the label that described it.

We believe the way things are chosen still matters.

A merchant house is not a brand. It is a practice. The act of selecting, judging, and staking your name on what you sell. Every day.

House of Singhana exists because someone in this family decided that this practice should not disappear.

The Selection

Three things. Selected by judgment. Not variety — conviction.

01

Turmeric

Most turmeric is sold on colour alone. A bright yellow tells the buyer nothing about what is inside.

We select on three criteria: colour, aroma, and curcumin density. If it fails any one of the three, it goes back.

A merchant learns to judge aroma before appearance. That is the difference between selling turmeric and knowing turmeric.

02

Chilli

Chilli is not one thing. Different dishes need different heat.

For everyday cooking Balanced heat. Consistent colour. The one that works in everything.
For heat High capsaicin. Sharp, direct warmth.
For colour and aroma Low heat. Deep crimson. For the dish that needs to look right.

A merchant who knows the product does not sell everything under one name.

03

Coriander

The spice most people do not notice until it is wrong.

Ground well, it adds a citrus-clean base that holds everything else together. Ground carelessly, it tastes like dust.

We grind at low temperature to preserve the volatile oils. The difference is noticeable in the first thirty seconds of cooking.

The selection. Turmeric. Chilli. Coriander.
